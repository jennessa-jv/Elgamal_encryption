ElGamal, one step at a time

Alice publishes a public key. Bob uses it to lock a message. Only Alice’s private number can unlock it. Change any value below and watch every calculation update.

Background and history

  • ElGamal is based on discrete logarithms and is closely related to the Diffie–Hellman technique.
  • Applications: digital signature standards (DSS) and S/MIME email standards.
  1. 1976: Diffie–Hellman key exchangeWhitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman show that two people can agree on a shared secret over an open channel, using modular exponentiation and the discrete logarithm problem. It is the mathematical seed of ElGamal.
  2. 1977–78: RSARivest, Shamir and Adleman publish the first practical public-key system, built on factoring instead of discrete logs.
  3. 1984–85: Taher ElGamal’s paperA student of Hellman at Stanford, Taher ElGamal turns Diffie–Hellman into a full encryption scheme plus a signature scheme. It was published in 1985 in IEEE Transactions on Information Theory.
  4. Standards: DSS and S/MIMEThe scheme’s discrete-log ideas appear in digital signature standards (DSS) and in S/MIME email standards. DSS specifies its own signature algorithm (DSA), which is related to ElGamal signatures but not identical, and not every S/MIME system uses ElGamal.
Start from

Global public elements: q is a prime number, and α is a primitive root of q with α < q.

Alice

1. Make a key pair

Alice computes yA = αxA mod q, then publishes (q, α, yA) and keeps xA to herself.

Why can’t anyone just work out xA?

Going forward is quick. Going backward means solving the discrete logarithm problem: given yA, find xA. With a tiny q like 23 that’s easy by trial; with a prime of 2000+ bits it is not feasible.

Bob

2. Encrypt with Alice’s public key

Bob picks a fresh random k, then sends the pair (C1, C2). He never needs xA. K acts as a one-time key used to encrypt and decrypt the message.

Alice

3. Decrypt with the private key

Alice rebuilds the shared secret K from C1 and xA, undoes the multiplication with K’s inverse, and gets M back. You can edit the ciphertext to see what happens.
